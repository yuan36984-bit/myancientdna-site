What Does "Trace" Ancestry Mean in DNA Results?

Guide · Updated 2026

A small percentage tucked at the bottom of an ancestry or ancient-origins report — often labeled "trace region," "trace ancestry," or shown at 1% or less — tends to draw outsized attention. Here's what that small number is actually telling you, and where its limits are.

Typical thresholdMost providers label results below roughly 1-2% as "trace," though the exact cutoff varies by company
What it reflectsA genuine but small and lower-confidence statistical signal linking part of your DNA to a specific reference population
Main causesA distant ancestor from that population, statistical noise near the detection threshold, or shared deep ancestry between neighboring reference populations
How stable it isLess stable than large ancestry percentages — trace results are the most likely to shift or disappear when a provider updates its reference panel

Why trace results exist at all

Ancestry estimation works by comparing segments of your DNA to reference populations and assigning each segment a best statistical match. Most segments cluster clearly into your main ancestry groups. But a small number of segments can be genuinely ambiguous or can reflect a distant ancestor from an outside population — and when that happens consistently enough across your genome, the algorithm reports it as a small "trace" percentage rather than folding it into your main results.

Three reasons a trace result shows up

A trace percentage generally comes from one of three sources, and providers can't always tell you which one applies to your specific case:

  • A real, distant ancestor. Someone several generations back may have belonged to that population, but random inheritance means only a small fragment of their DNA survived down to you.
  • Shared ancestry between neighboring groups. Reference populations that lived near each other or share deep ancestry can produce small cross-matches even without a specific ancestor from that group.
  • Statistical noise. Near the lower detection threshold, small amounts of measurement uncertainty can produce a trace result that doesn't correspond to a real, distinct ancestral connection at all.

How this applies to ancient-origins results specifically

The same logic applies when a small ancient population result appears in an ancient-origins panel rather than a standard modern ethnicity estimate. Because ancient reference populations are compared over a much longer timespan, a trace-level match to an ancient population is often reflecting deep, shared ancestral connections between regions — rather than a specific recent ancestor — more often than a trace result in a modern ethnicity estimate would.

How to weigh a trace result

A trace result becomes more meaningful when it's consistent with independent evidence: known family history, documented genealogical records, or a consistent appearance across updates to the same provider's algorithm over time. On its own, a single small trace percentage with no supporting evidence is best treated as a tentative signal rather than a settled fact about your ancestry.

For the bigger picture on how any of these percentages — trace or otherwise — are calculated in the first place, see our ancient DNA explainer. If a specific ancient population shows up in your trace results, browse the full ancient populations directory to see what that group means.

Should I ignore trace ancestry results entirely?

Not necessarily, but treat them as a lead rather than a conclusion. A trace result that lines up with known family history or other documented records is worth taking seriously; an unexpected trace result with no other supporting evidence is best treated as tentative.

Why did a trace result I had before disappear after an update?

This is common and expected. As a provider's reference panel and algorithm improve, small low-confidence estimates are often the first results to shift, since they were closest to the noise floor to begin with.
