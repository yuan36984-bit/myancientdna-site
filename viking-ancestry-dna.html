Viking-Era Scandinavian Ancestry: What It Means

Ancient population guide · Updated 2026

Viking-era Scandinavian ancestry is one of the most requested categories in Ancient Origins results — and one of the most misunderstood. Large-scale ancient-DNA sequencing of Viking-age skeletons has shown that the people we call "Vikings" were far more genetically diverse than the popular image suggests.

Also calledNorse Viking ancestry, Viking Age ancestry
Time periodRoughly 793–1066 CE (the Viking Age), falling within MyHeritage's broader Middle Ages era
HomelandScandinavia (modern Denmark, Norway, Sweden), with regional variation in raiding/settlement patterns
Key studyMargaryan et al., "Population genomics of the Viking world" (Nature, 2020) — 442 ancient genomes analyzed
Notable individualThe Birka female warrior (grave Bj 581), confirmed genetically female in a 2017 study

Who were the Viking-era Scandinavians?

The largest ancient-DNA study of the Viking world to date, published in Nature in 2020, sequenced 442 genomes from Viking-age skeletons across Europe and Greenland. It found that Viking-age Scandinavia was not a genetically isolated or homogeneous population — there was substantial gene flow into Scandinavia from Southern Europe and Asia even before the Viking Age began, and the study identified individuals buried with Viking cultural artifacts who had significant Sami, British, or Irish ancestry. The study also found meaningful genetic differences between Danish, Norwegian, and Swedish Viking populations, generally lining up with their different raiding and settlement patterns: Danish Vikings toward England and Western Europe, Norwegian Vikings toward Ireland, Scotland, Iceland, and Greenland, and Swedish Vikings eastward toward Russia and the Byzantine world.

How Viking-era ancestry shows up in modern populations

Because Viking-age raiding, trading, and settlement reached from North America to Central Asia, genetic traces of this period turn up well beyond Scandinavia — in parts of the British Isles, Normandy, Iceland, and along former trade routes into Eastern Europe. Modern Scandinavians naturally carry the highest concentrations of this ancestry, but it's also detectable, in smaller proportions, across a wider swath of Northern Europe due to the reach of Viking-age settlement and intermarriage.

If your Ancient Origins results show Viking-era ancestry

A Viking-era or Middle Ages match with a Scandinavian profile reflects resemblance to the ancient genomes sampled from this period, not confirmation of a specific saga-worthy lineage. Given how genetically mixed the Viking world actually was, this category can appear in results with several different regional backgrounds — not only people of Scandinavian descent. MyHeritage's Notable Individuals report (available on Complete and Omni subscriptions) also compares your DNA to specific historical figures from this era, including the Birka female warrior.

Viking-era ancestry vs. Iron Age Celtic ancestry

Viking-age raiding and settlement overlapped heavily with regions that had long-established Celtic-associated populations, particularly in Ireland, Scotland, and parts of England. Many people with British or Irish ancestry today carry a mix of both signals, reflecting centuries of Viking-age settlement layered on top of the existing Celtic and Anglo-Saxon population base rather than a clean replacement of one by the other.

Looking specifically at Norwegian ancestry?

Of the three Viking-age Scandinavian populations, Norwegian Vikings are the ones most closely tied to the westward voyages that reached Scotland, Ireland, Iceland, and Greenland. If your family history points specifically to Norway rather than Scandinavia more broadly, see our dedicated guide to Norwegian DNA ancestry, which covers haplogroup I1, Sámi admixture in the north, and how Norwegian results relate to Swedish and Danish ones.

Which DNA test picks up Viking-era ancestry best?

Not every DNA test reports on this category the same way. If you specifically want strong Viking-era or Norse detection, see our breakdown of the best DNA test for Viking ancestry, comparing how MyHeritage, AncestryDNA, LivingDNA, and FamilyTreeDNA each handle this period.

Does Viking ancestry mean my ancestors were raiders?

Not necessarily — the Viking Age involved far more trade, settlement, and intermarriage than the popular "raiders and warriors" image suggests. Ancient-DNA studies show many people buried with Viking-associated artifacts and grave goods had varied roles and diverse genetic backgrounds, not a single warrior archetype.

Why did Viking-age Scandinavia have so much genetic diversity?

Research published in Nature found evidence of gene flow into Scandinavia from Southern Europe and Asia predating the Viking Age itself, plus ongoing mixing with populations the Vikings encountered through raiding, trade, and settlement across the British Isles, Continental Europe, and further east — all of which left a detectable mark in the genomes of people buried with Viking-era artifacts.
