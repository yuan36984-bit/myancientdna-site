Early European Farmer Ancestry: What It Means

Ancient population guide · Updated 2026

Early European Farmer ancestry traces back to one of the most transformative events in human history: the spread of agriculture out of Anatolia and the Near East into Europe, starting around 6000 BCE. If this population shows up prominently in your Ancient Origins results, it links you to the communities that first brought crop cultivation and animal domestication to the European continent.

Also calledAnatolian farmer ancestry, Neolithic farmer ancestry, EEF
Time periodRoughly 8000–4000 BCE, spreading into Europe from about 6000 BCE onward
HomelandAnatolia (modern Turkey) and the wider Fertile Crescent
IntroducedCereal farming, animal domestication, permanent settlements
Strongest today inThe Mediterranean, especially Sardinia

Who were the Early European Farmers?

Agriculture — the cultivation of crops like wheat and barley, and the domestication of animals such as sheep, goats, and cattle — first developed in the Fertile Crescent and Anatolia. Starting around 6000 BCE, communities carrying this Anatolian farmer ancestry began migrating into Europe along two main corridors: one following the Mediterranean coastline, and one moving up the Danube river valley into Central Europe, associated with the archaeological Linearbandkeramik (LBK) culture known for its distinctive incised pottery. This is often described as the Neolithic Revolution — one of the most significant lifestyle shifts in human history, moving communities from foraging to permanent, farming-based settlement.

How Early European Farmer ancestry shows up in modern populations

As farming populations spread across Europe, they gradually admixed with the hunter-gatherer groups already living there, though in many regions farmer ancestry became the dominant genetic component for centuries. That dominance was later diluted in parts of Europe — especially the north — by the arrival of steppe pastoralists during the Bronze Age. Because that later steppe migration had a smaller relative impact on Mediterranean populations, islands like Sardinia are frequently cited in genetics research as having unusually high, well-preserved Early European Farmer ancestry compared to the rest of the continent.

If your Ancient Origins results show Early European Farmer ancestry

A strong Early European Farmer signal suggests your ancestry draws heavily on populations that were established in Europe before the Bronze Age steppe migrations reshaped much of the gene pool — this tends to be especially pronounced for people with Southern European or Mediterranean ancestry. As always, treat the exact percentage as a statistical estimate rather than a precise figure — MyHeritage's Ancient Origins dashboard recalculates results periodically as its reference panel of ancient samples expands.

Early European Farmer ancestry vs. Steppe ancestry

Together with Bronze Age Steppe ancestry, this forms the fundamental two-part story of most Europeans' deep ancestry: an earlier farming population established across the continent from the Neolithic onward, followed by a later, substantial demographic shift from steppe pastoralists arriving from the east. Neither category "belongs" to one modern nationality — both are spread, in varying proportions, across most of Europe today.

Is Early European Farmer ancestry the same as being Anatolian or Turkish today?

No. It reflects genetic descent from Neolithic-era populations that lived in Anatolia roughly 8,000–10,000 years ago, before later population movements reshaped the region. It has no direct bearing on modern national or ethnic identity in present-day Turkey or anywhere else.

Why is Sardinia mentioned so often in farmer-ancestry research?

Sardinia's relative geographic isolation meant it received less of the later Bronze Age steppe migration that reshaped much of mainland Europe's ancestry. As a result, its population has retained an unusually well-preserved genetic profile close to that of early Neolithic farmers, making it a frequently studied reference point in ancient-DNA research.
