Ancient Egyptian Ancestry: What It Means

Ancient population guide · Updated 2026

Ancient Egypt is one of the longest-running civilizations included in Ancient Origins comparisons, and one of the best-documented by ancient-DNA research thanks to a landmark mummy-genome study published in 2017.

Time span studiedRoughly 1400 BCE – 400 CE (New Kingdom through Roman-era Egypt)
Key studySchuenemann et al., "Ancient Egyptian mummy genomes..." (Nature Communications, 2017)
Sample sourceAbout 90 mummies from Abusir el-Meleq, Middle Egypt
Closest ancient relativesAncient Near Eastern and Levantine populations
Key findingSub-Saharan African ancestry in the region increased notably after the Roman period

Who were the ancient Egyptians?

A widely cited 2017 study in Nature Communications sequenced mitochondrial and, for a smaller subset, nuclear genomes from roughly 90 Egyptian mummies excavated at Abusir el-Meleq in Middle Egypt, spanning around 1,300 years from the New Kingdom period through Roman rule. The study found that these ancient Egyptians were most closely genetically related to ancient populations from the Near East and the Levant — broadly consistent with Egypt's geographic position as a crossroads between Africa, the Middle East, and the Mediterranean. Because the site is in Middle Egypt and the samples cluster in a specific window of time, the researchers were careful to note the results may not fully represent the genetic diversity of ancient Egypt across its full ~3,000-year history or its full geographic range from the Nile Delta to Upper Egypt and Nubia.

2025 update: the first whole genome from Old Kingdom Egypt

In July 2025, researchers published the first whole-genome sequence recovered from an individual who lived during Egypt's Old Kingdom — roughly 4,500 to 4,800 years ago, within the era of the pyramid builders (around the 3rd to 6th Dynasty, c. 2686–2181 BCE). The genome came from a man buried at Nuwayrat in Middle Egypt, whose remains were well enough preserved for whole-genome sequencing, a rarer feat than the mitochondrial and partial-genome work behind the 2017 mummy study above. His ancestry was found to be predominantly related to ancient North African populations, with roughly a fifth of his ancestry best matching ancient populations from Mesopotamia — an early genetic signal of contact between Egypt and the Fertile Crescent long before the Greco-Roman period covered elsewhere on this page. Researchers described the result as a milestone after roughly four decades of attempts to recover usable ancient DNA from Egyptian remains, where heat and burial conditions usually degrade DNA quickly. If you see this study referenced in discussions of your own Ancient Origins results, keep in mind it reflects one individual from one site and time period, not a general profile for all of ancient Egypt.

How ancient Egyptian ancestry shows up in modern populations

The same 2017 study found that present-day Egyptians carry meaningfully more sub-Saharan African ancestry than the ancient mummy samples did — a shift the researchers linked to increased mobility along the Nile and expanded trans-Saharan trade networks in the centuries following the Roman period, rather than to a sudden single event. This makes ancient Egyptian ancestry a useful example of how a population's genetic profile can shift gradually over centuries even within the same geographic region, long after the civilization most associated with it had ended.

Given ancient Egypt's close genetic relationship to Near Eastern and Levantine populations, people with ancestry from the broader Eastern Mediterranean and Middle East may also see some resemblance in this category. Ancient Egyptian ancestry vs. Imperial Roman ancestry

Egypt became a Roman province in 30 BCE and remained one of the empire's most important territories — a major grain supplier and trade hub — for centuries afterward, which is part of why the ancient Egyptian mummy-genome samples span all the way into the Roman era. See our page on Imperial Roman ancestry for how Roman-era Mediterranean populations compare more broadly to this specific Egyptian lineage.

Does ancient Egyptian ancestry mean I'm related to the pharaohs?

Not specifically. The reference samples behind this category come from mummies at a particular burial site, representing the general population of that time and place rather than royal lineages. Royal Egyptian genomes, including any belonging to specific pharaohs, are a separate and much more limited area of research.

Why do modern Egyptians have different ancestry than ancient mummies studied in 2017?

The study's authors linked the difference to increased genetic contact with sub-Saharan Africa in the centuries after the Roman period, likely driven by expanded trade and mobility along the Nile and across the Sahara — a gradual demographic shift rather than a single migration event.
