What Does Your Neanderthal DNA Percentage Mean?

Guide · Updated 2026

Most DNA testing services that report Neanderthal ancestry show a single number — typically somewhere between under 1% and around 4% of your genome. That number is real and statistically meaningful, but it's easy to misread. Here's what it actually reflects, and what it doesn't.

Typical rangeUnder 1% to roughly 4% of the genome, for people of non-African ancestry
Who has zeroPopulations with entirely Sub-Saharan African ancestry generally show little to no detectable Neanderthal DNA, since the interbreeding occurred after early modern humans left Africa
What it's measuringThe proportion of your genome made up of DNA segments statistically identified as Neanderthal-derived, based on comparison to sequenced Neanderthal reference genomes
What it's notNot a measure of "how Neanderthal you look" or any physical trait — the vast majority of Neanderthal-derived segments have no established effect on appearance

Where the percentage comes from

Roughly 50,000-60,000 years ago, as early modern humans migrated out of Africa, they interbred with Neanderthal populations already established across Europe and Asia. That mixing left a small but detectable and permanent signature in the DNA of everyone descended from those migrating populations. A testing algorithm identifies this signature by comparing your genome, segment by segment, against sequenced ancient Neanderthal genomes and looking for stretches of DNA that match the Neanderthal reference more closely than they match other modern human reference populations.

Why your number isn't the same as anyone else's

DNA is inherited in randomly shuffled chunks — a process called recombination — from each parent, generation after generation. That means the specific Neanderthal-derived segments you carry, and how many of them survived tens of thousands of years of random inheritance without being shuffled out, is somewhat a matter of chance. Two unrelated people of similar ancestry can reasonably have noticeably different Neanderthal DNA percentages, and even full siblings can differ from each other for the same reason.

A higher number doesn't mean "more Neanderthal ancestry" in a simple sense

Because everyone with non-African ancestry descends from the same original interbreeding events, a higher percentage doesn't indicate a more recent or more direct Neanderthal ancestor — it just reflects that more Neanderthal-derived segments happened to survive the shuffling process in your particular lineage. It's a difference of degree within a shared ancestral event, not a difference in kind.

Does the percentage do anything?

Research has linked a small number of specific Neanderthal-derived gene variants to traits like immune response, skin and hair characteristics, and altitude adaptation in some populations — but these effects are tied to specific segments, not to your overall percentage. Two people with the same total percentage can carry a completely different mix of functional and non-functional segments.

Curious about the discovery and sequencing history behind this comparison in the first place? See our full Neanderthal DNA explainer. If you're more interested in a related but separate archaic population, see our Denisovan DNA guide — some ancient-origins panels report both.

Is a higher Neanderthal DNA percentage unusual?

Most people of non-African ancestry fall somewhere in the 1-2% range, with the tested range extending roughly from under 1% to around 4%. A result toward the higher end of that range isn't a data error — it reflects normal variation in how much Neanderthal-derived DNA was inherited and retained through thousands of generations.

Why do siblings sometimes get different Neanderthal DNA percentages?

Because DNA is inherited in randomly shuffled segments from each parent, full siblings can inherit somewhat different sets of Neanderthal-derived segments, producing slightly different percentages even though both parents are the same.
