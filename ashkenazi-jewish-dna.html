Ashkenazi Jewish Ancestry: What It Means

Ancient population guide · Updated 2026

Ashkenazi Jews of the Middle Ages are named specifically among the populations covered by MyHeritage's Ancient Origins feature. Population genetics research has traced this group's origins to a distinctive combination of ancient Middle Eastern roots and centuries of history in medieval Europe — plus a genetic bottleneck that makes Ashkenazi ancestry unusually well-documented compared to many other historical populations.

FormedPrimarily in medieval Central and later Eastern Europe, from roughly 1000 CE onward
Dual ancestryAncient Levantine/Middle Eastern roots combined with substantial Southern European admixture

Who were the Ashkenazi Jews of the Middle Ages?

Ashkenazi Jewish communities took shape in medieval Europe, first concentrated in the Rhineland region of Central Europe before later generations moved eastward into Poland, Lithuania, and the broader Russian Empire from roughly the 12th century onward. Population genetics studies, including a widely cited 2014 sequencing project published in Nature Communications, have found that Ashkenazi Jewish ancestry reflects a distinctive mixture: shared genetic roots with the ancient Levant and Middle East, consistent with historical accounts of Jewish diaspora communities dispersing from the region of ancient Israel and Judea, combined with substantial admixture from European populations — particularly Southern European — accumulated through conversion and intermarriage over the following centuries.

How Ashkenazi Jewish ancestry shows up in modern populations

Genetic studies have identified a significant population bottleneck in Ashkenazi Jewish history — a period, estimated at roughly 600 to 800 years ago, when the founding population is thought to have numbered only in the hundreds before expanding rapidly. Combined with centuries of relative endogamy (marriage within the community), this bottleneck left Ashkenazi Jewish populations today unusually genetically distinct and internally consistent compared to many other historical groups, which is part of why they're such a well-studied population in genetics research — including for medically relevant founder-effect conditions such as certain BRCA gene variants and Tay-Sachs disease.

If your Ancient Origins results show Ashkenazi Jewish ancestry

A strong match to this category reflects genetic resemblance to the historical Ashkenazi Jewish population that formed in medieval Europe, carrying its characteristic blend of ancient Levantine and Southern European ancestry. Because of the population's well-documented bottleneck and relative genetic distinctiveness, this is one of the more clearly identifiable signals in an ancient-DNA comparison. Ashkenazi Jewish ancestry vs. Imperial Roman ancestry

The Jewish diaspora that eventually gave rise to Ashkenazi communities in medieval Europe has roots stretching back to the Roman period and earlier, including the Roman-era Levant. See our page on Imperial Roman ancestry for context on the broader Mediterranean and Near Eastern population movements that preceded the Ashkenazi community's formation in Central Europe centuries later.

Is Ashkenazi Jewish ancestry a religious category or a genetic one?

Ancient Origins compares your DNA to a genetic reference population, not a religious affiliation. The Ashkenazi Jewish reference population reflects a historically and genetically distinct group shaped by shared Levantine ancestry, medieval European admixture, and centuries of relative endogamy — someone can carry this ancestry regardless of their own religious practice or upbringing.

Why is Ashkenazi ancestry linked to certain hereditary health conditions?

Because of the population's genetic bottleneck — a relatively small founding group several centuries ago followed by rapid growth within a largely endogamous community — certain gene variants that happened to be present in the founders became more common than they are in the general population, a phenomenon geneticists call a founder effect. This is why some hereditary conditions and cancer-risk gene variants appear at higher frequency in Ashkenazi populations specifically.
