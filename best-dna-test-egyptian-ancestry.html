Our take

If your interest is specifically "does my DNA resemble ancient Egyptians," MyHeritage's Ancient Origins is currently the only mainstream option that makes that direct archaeological comparison — see exactly what that category shows on our Ancient Egyptian ancestry page. AncestryDNA and 23andMe's "North Africa" or "Egypt" regions are useful for a modern regional picture, but they're comparing you to today's population, which published ancient-DNA research shows has shifted genetically over the last two thousand years due to later migration and trade — so a modern-region match isn't the same claim as an ancient-Egyptian match.

Why ancient and modern Egyptian DNA aren't the same thing

A widely cited 2017 study sequencing DNA from Abusir el-Meleq mummies (roughly 1400 BCE to 400 CE) found that ancient Egyptians were genetically closest to ancient populations of the Near East and eastern Mediterranean, with relatively little sub-Saharan African ancestry at that time. Sub-Saharan ancestry in the region increased measurably over the following centuries, likely through trans-Saharan trade, migration, and the Islamic expansion. That means a DNA test comparing you to modern Egyptians is measuring something meaningfully different from a comparison to Old Kingdom or New Kingdom-era ancient Egyptians — see our full Ancient Egyptian ancestry guide for the details.

FAQ

Can any DNA test prove I'm related to a specific pharaoh?

No consumer DNA test can trace a documented line to a specific named pharaoh. What Ancient Origins-style features show is a population-level statistical resemblance to sampled ancient Egyptian archaeological DNA, not a genealogical record connecting you to an individual historical figure.

Why does my "Egypt" or "North Africa" percentage differ from what I expected?

If your test only offers a modern-region breakdown, it's comparing your DNA to living populations in that region today, not to ancient Egyptian archaeological samples. Ancient and modern Egyptian populations differ genetically due to two thousand years of subsequent migration, so the two results answer different questions.
